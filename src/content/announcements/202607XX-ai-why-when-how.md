Strip away the mystique and the thing behind ChatGPT is a prediction engine: it writes one word at a time, each predicted from everything before it. That is why it can read ten thousand maintenance logs in moments, and why, when unsure, it does not go quiet but guesses fluently, with excellent grammar. The "agents" in the headlines are the same engines given permission to act. Think of a tirelessly confident junior analyst: reads everything, never sleeps, drafts in seconds, occasionally wrong. Several on a team raise output. None of them signs.

The evidence worth trusting comes from randomized controlled trials, the same design used to test medicines, not from vendor decks; the chart above is what they found. The detail that matters: the largest gains went to the weakest performers. Hiring lifts a team one person at a time. This lifts the whole floor at once, a lever executives have never had. The warning rarely makes a slide: on tasks outside the tool's competence, the same people were 19 percentage points **more** likely to get it wrong, because they trusted it past an edge they could not see. The gains and the failure mode arrive together. Designing for the second is the most consistent habit of the few who earn from the first.

## THE WHEN

Three questions decide it, and none is about technology. **Can you name the decision?** A real one that comes round every week and gets made late on partial information: what to quote, which asset gets the crew, where the money leaked last month. **Does the data behind it agree with itself?** Gartner expects 60% of AI projects to be abandoned over data that was never ready. If two departments calculate downtime differently, a model will not settle the argument; it will pick a side, with conviction. **Can you show a return inside a quarter?** Boards expect a technology investment to pay back in seven to twelve months; most organizations report AI taking two to four years (Deloitte). The gap is not the technology, it is value stacked at the end of one long program instead of small steps that pay as they go. Three yeses and you are readier than most of the 88%.


## THE HOW

Projects die of unowned decisions, unprepared data and undefined success, all settled before a model is ever chosen; last year 42% of organizations abandoned most of their AI initiatives (S&P Global). What the successful few do instead is unglamorous. **Plumbing first.** Most of what executives want from "AI" is arithmetic on joined-up data: profit per site per day, labor against the revenue it produced. Once the plumbing exists those numbers arrive on their own, in weeks, and they pay for the foundation. **Models only where arithmetic cannot reach:** forecasting, anomalies, messy free text. Everything else is a report. **Define "working" before development starts** and attach a business number, or it will not survive its first budget review. **Own your data even when you rent the software:** live access, bulk export, exit terms in writing, agreed before signature rather than bought back after. ## THE BOTTOM LINE

This month's headlines, Alphabet raising its capital spending guidance for the year to $205 billion, Nvidia in deal talks worth a combined three-quarters of a trillion dollars, say nothing about whether AI works in your business. They say the supply side is going to reprice and consolidate, and that the only things you will still hold afterwards are the ones you own: your data, in a foundation you control, and a decision you can name. Build on those and a vendor shakeout is a procurement exercise. Build on a vendor and it is an outage. *(Reuters, Bloomberg, New York Times, 20 to 28 July 2026.)*

So take the test. **Name the decision.** If you can, then AI, or often just joined-up data and honest arithmetic, has a place in your business, and a first project shows something measurable inside a quarter. If you cannot, no model will rescue you; the honest first step is plumbing, not a pilot. Look again at Figure 1: every organization in that survey had the same models. Choosing one was never the hard part, and it has never, on its own, made anyone any money.
