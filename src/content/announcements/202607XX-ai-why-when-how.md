Almost everyone is using AI but very few are getting **meaningful returns.** In our experience the difference has little to do with budget and nothing to do with the AI model picked. 

It has to do with these three questions: 1\) **Why** should we take AI seriously, beyond the headlines? 2\) **When** does it make sense to use it? 3\) **How** do we start with it?

This article addresses these important questions. It is written for people who run their businesses and are curious about using AI to make it better. Perhaps the statistic below is a good place to start:

To look at these questions with a deeper lens, let us take a minute to understand what AI actually is. Strip away the mystique and the thing behind ChatGPT and its cousins is a “prediction engine”. It has read an enormous amount of text, and it writes answers one word at a time, each word predicted from everything that came before it. This one fact explains both the magic and the trouble.

The magic is **speed and breadth**. Something that predicts language this well can read ten thousand maintenance logs in a few moments and tell you what is in them. It can draft a contract clause. It can line up four systems that have never once agreed on what a "department" is.

The trouble is that a prediction engine does not know things the way the person who closes your books knows them, from a record they can point to. When it is unsure, it does not go quiet. It guesses fluently, in complete sentences, with excellent grammar. This is not a reason to walk away from it. It is simply how the machine is built, and it is why anything built seriously shows *its sources.*

AI “agents” we hear a lot about are the same “prediction engines” given permission to act: look something up, fill in a form, kick off a report. Genuinely useful. It is also why oversight matters more rather than less, because a system that can do things needs closer monitoring. The analogy that best encompasses this is to think of an AI agent as a tirelessly confident junior analyst \- reads everything, never sleeps, drafts in seconds, and is occasionally wrong. However, you would not let this analyst take any decision without oversight, though having multiples of them on the team would increase productivity.


## THE WHY

Caption: Figure 2. Sources: Harvard Business School and BCG randomized controlled trial, 758 consultants, 2023. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2025 (peer reviewed).


The evidence worth trusting here does not come from vendor decks. It comes from randomized controlled trials, the same design used to test medicines. In them, people given an AI assistant worked about 25% faster and produced work rated 40% better, and the largest gains went to the weakest performers: roughly 43% improvement for the bottom half of the group against 17% for the top. Hiring and training lift a team one person at a time. This lifts the whole floor at once, which is a lever most executives have never had. The same trials carry a warning that rarely makes it onto a slide. When the task fell outside what the tool was good at, those same people were 19 percentage points **more** likely to get it wrong, because they could not see where the edge was and trusted it past the edge. The gains and the failure mode arrive together. You do not get the 25% and the 40% without designing for the 19, and designing for the 19 is the most consistent habit of the few who are earning from this.

*(You have probably also heard that 95% of enterprise AI pilots return nothing. That figure comes from a preliminary working paper: not peer reviewed, with failure defined narrowly as no quick profit-and-loss impact, published by a group that also builds the sort of infrastructure it recommends. The direction is right. The precision is not. We would rather you had the honest version of that number than the viral one.)*

Three questions decide this, and not one of them is about technology. **First, can you name the decision?** Not "we should look at AI", but a real one that comes round every week and currently gets made late, on stale or partial information: which jobs to bid, what to quote, which asset gets the crew, where the money leaked last month. **Second, does the data behind that decision agree with itself?** The numbers that matter usually sit in four or five systems that were never designed to talk to each other, which is why Gartner expects 60% of AI projects to be abandoned over data that was never ready, and why 63% of data leaders say they either lack the right practices or do not know whether they have them. Putting AI on top of fragmented data does not fix the fragmentation, it produces confident nonsense faster. If two departments calculate downtime differently, the model will not settle the argument. It will pick a side, with conviction. **Third, can you show a return inside a quarter?** Most organizations report two to four years to a satisfactory return, against the seven to twelve months a board expects of a normal technology investment, and only 6% see payback inside a year. That gap is not the nature of the technology. It is what happens when the value is stacked at the far end of one long transformation program. Sequenced as small steps, the same work pays as it goes, and the first joined-up report often settles a question leadership has argued about for a year. Answer yes to all three and you are already readier than most of the 88%.

Projects die of unowned decisions, unprepared data and undefined success, every one of which is settled before a model is ever chosen. Note that the bars above are split on purpose: the solid ones happened to real projects, the hatched ones are analyst forecasts, and anyone showing you all four as the same kind of fact is doing something to you rather than for you. What the successful few do about it is unglamorous and very learnable. **Plumbing first**, because most of what executives want from "AI" turns out to be arithmetic on joined-up data, profit per site per day, labor against the revenue it produced, cost against benchmark, and once the plumbing exists those numbers arrive on their own. That is usually the first win, it lands in weeks, and it pays for the foundation before any model shows up. **Models only where arithmetic cannot reach:** forecasting, anomalies, patterns in messy free text, pricing under uncertainty. Everything else is a report. **Define what "working" means before development starts** and attach a business number to it, because a result nobody can measure cannot be scaled and will not survive its first budget review. **Own your data even when you rent the software**, and ask the portability questions before your next renewal rather than after: live access to your own data, the right to pull your history in bulk, exit terms in writing. Asked before signature, those are contract terms. Asked afterwards, they are change requests at the supplier's price. And **run the work as a sequence of gates, each with an exit.**

## THE BOTTOM LINE

One more thing, plainly, because it is in every headline this month. In the last ten days of July 2026, Alphabet told investors its capital spending this year could reach $205 billion, up from the $190 billion ceiling it had given a quarter earlier, which is a very large company saying out loud that it cannot forecast its own costs. Nvidia has been in rounds of deal talks worth a combined three-quarters of a trillion dollars, which one sector strategist called as much a reminder of funding strain in the build-out as a signal of demand for it. A Chinese lab shipped another competitive model without the chips it is supposed to need. Even the investors most bullish on all of this expect data centers to be overbuilt and a fair number of AI companies to disappear in the correction. None of that tells you whether AI works in your business. What it tells you is that the supply side is going to reprice and consolidate, probably more than once, and that the only things you will still hold afterwards are the ones you own: your data, in a foundation you control, and a decision you can name. Build on those and a vendor shakeout is a procurement exercise. Build on a vendor and it is an outage. *(Reuters, Bloomberg and the New York Times, 20 to 28 July 2026.)*

So take the test. **Name the decision.** If you can name it, then AI, or quite often just joined-up data and honest arithmetic, has a place in your business, and a first project scopes in weeks with something measurable to show for it inside the quarter. If you cannot name it, no model will rescue you, and the honest first step is plumbing rather than a pilot. Then look again at Figure 1. 88% of organizations use AI regularly and about 6% can point to it moving earnings by more than five percent, and the distance between those two numbers is not a technology gap. Every organization in that survey had access to the same models. Choosing the model was never the hard part. It is also the part that has never, on its own, made anyone any money.
